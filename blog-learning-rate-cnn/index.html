How Learning Rate Shapes the Training Curve of a Small CNN

One CNN, four learning rates, everything else held fixed

November 16, 2026

If you only get to tune one hyperparameter, most people will tell you to tune the learning rate. I wanted to see what that advice looks like in practice. So I took one small CNN, trained it four times on Fashion-MNIST, and changed nothing between the runs except the learning rate. How does the learning rate change the training and validation curves of a small CNN, and where does it go from “too slow” to “broken”?

Short answer: across a 1000× range, I got everything from a network that was still crawling after 20 epochs, to one that hit 80% validation accuracy in two epochs, to one that gave up completely and guessed at random.

What the learning rate actually does¶

Training a network means adjusting its weights www to make a loss L(w)L(w)L(w) smaller. The loss measures how wrong the predictions are; here it is cross-entropy. The gradient ∇L(w)\nabla L(w)∇L(w) points in the direction where the loss grows fastest. So we step the other way:

wt+1=wt−η ∇L(wt)w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta \, \nabla L(w_t)wt+1​=wt​−η∇L(wt​)
(1)

That is plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD). “Stochastic” means the gradient is estimated from a small random batch of images instead of the whole dataset. The learning rate η\etaη (eta) is the size of that step. The gradient tells you which way is downhill, and the learning rate decides how far you walk before checking again.

Why does the step size matter so much? Take the simplest possible loss, a parabola L(w)=a2w2L(w) = \tfrac{a}{2} w^2L(w)=2a​w2, whose minimum is at w=0w = 0w=0. Its gradient is awa waw, so one SGD step gives

wt+1=wt−ηawt=(1−ηa) wt.w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta a w_t = (1 - \eta a)\, w_t .wt+1​=wt​−ηawt​=(1−ηa)wt​.
(2)

Each step multiplies the distance from the minimum by (1−ηa)(1 - \eta a)(1−ηa), which gives three regimes:

  • η\etaη small (ηa\eta aηa close to 0): the factor is just under 1, so you move toward the minimum, but very slowly.

  • η\etaη in the sweet spot (0<ηa<20 < \eta a < 20<ηa<2): the factor’s size is below 1, so you converge. Past ηa=1\eta a = 1ηa=1 you overshoot and bounce from side to side, but each bounce is smaller.

  • η\etaη too big (ηa>2\eta a > 2ηa>2): the factor’s size is above 1, so every step lands further from the minimum than the last. Training diverges.

A real network’s loss surface has millions of dimensions with different curvature in each, but the same three regimes still show up roughly. My experiment looks for them on a real model.

Experiment setup¶

I kept everything fixed except η\etaη:

SettingValue
DatasetFashion-MNIST (28×28 grayscale clothing images, 10 classes)
Data used10,000 training and 5,000 validation images, both drawn from the official 60k training set. The 10k test set was held out.
Model2 conv layers (16 and 32 channels, 3×3, ReLU, max-pool) + dense layer of 64 + output layer. 105,866 parameters.
OptimizerPlain SGD: no momentum, no weight decay, no learning-rate schedule
Learning rates compared0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0
Batch size / epochs / seed64 / 20 / 42
HardwareLaptop CPU, about 25–45 seconds per run

I used a subset so each run takes under a minute on a CPU. The learning rates are spaced by factors of 10 because their effect works on orders of magnitude.

To make the comparison fair, every run starts from the same initial weights and sees the same batches in the same order. Only the step size differs:

torch.manual_seed(SEED)                              # identical starting weights
model = SmallCNN()

After each epoch I record training loss, validation loss and validation accuracy. I picked the best learning rate using validation accuracy only. I then evaluated that single model on the test set once, at the end. Full code: lr_experiment.py on GitHub.

Discussion¶

Mostly what the theory predicted. The small rate was stable but slow, the middle rates converged, and the largest broke training. As the parabola suggests, the best value I tried (0.1) sat just below one that failed (1.0).

Divergence looked different than I expected. I assumed η=1.0\eta = 1.0η=1.0 would make the loss blow up to infinity or NaN. Instead it went flat at 2.306, right at the chance line. To find out why, I ran one epoch at each rate and counted ReLU units that output zero for every validation image. At η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1: none. At η=1.0\eta = 1.0η=1.0: 84% of the second conv layer’s channels and 97% of the dense layer’s units were dead. The output was identical for every image. A ReLU outputs max⁡(0,x)\max(0, x)max(0,x), and its gradient is zero when x<0x < 0x<0. The first few huge steps pushed most units into that negative zone, so they get no gradient and never recover. The network ends up predicting the same thing for every input, and its loss settles at ln 10. In a deep ReLU network, “too large” often means dead rather than exploding.

Bigger steps, noisier curves. In Figure 2 the η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1 curve swings by about 6 points between neighbouring epochs in mid-training. For example, it drops from 85.5% at epoch 5 to 79.5% at epoch 6. Each SGD step uses only 64 images, so every step contains some noise. A larger learning rate amplifies that noise. Both η=0.01\eta = 0.01η=0.01 and η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1 also drop sharply at epoch 20: 0.1 falls from 88.6% to 75.5%. The two runs saw exactly the same batches, so I suspect the last few batches of epoch 20 pulled both models the same wrong way. I haven’t tested that. The practical lesson is that choosing by final-epoch accuracy would have made 0.1 and 0.01 look tied at about 75.5%. Keeping the best validation checkpoint avoids that trap.

Limitations.

  • One seed per setting. Given how noisy the curves are, differences of a point or two between runs are not reliable. Repeating with 3–5 seeds and plotting the mean would fix this.

  • Only 20 epochs on a 10k subset. The η=0.001\eta = 0.001η=0.001 run was clearly not finished. With enough epochs it would likely close part of the gap, so “worse” here partly means “slower”.

  • A coarse grid. I only tried powers of 10. The real best value could be anywhere between 0.1 and 1.0.

  • Plain SGD only. Momentum, Adam or a learning-rate schedule (shrinking η\etaη over time) would all change where the sweet spot is. A schedule would probably also smooth out the late-epoch swings.

Takeaways¶

  1. Search the learning rate on a log scale first. With nothing else changed, moving from 0.001 to 0.1 took best validation accuracy from 72.5% to 88.6%. Try 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0, then refine around the best one.

  2. Look at the curve, not just the final number. A flat loss stuck at ln(number of classes) means the network has stopped learning, often from dead ReLUs. A curve that keeps falling slowly means your rate is probably too small.

  3. With a large learning rate, keep the best checkpoint. Validation accuracy can swing by several points from one epoch to the next. Selecting on the best validation epoch, not the last one, gives a fairer comparison.
